Mah-Mah Khoto – The Children’s Battle with Rain

The sweet days of childhood echo like an old melody in the soul. When we lived on the southern side of the Alborz and Damavand mountains, in Tehran’s smoky and uncertain alleys, the moment spring or summer peeked over the horizon, our bodies, hearts, and souls would rush to Takam — that land on the northern side of the same mountains, where forested peaks lean on the sky and rivers tell silent tales.

For me, Takam was not just a village. It was a temple where worship was performed through laughter, games, and song. There I learned that rain, though a blessing, can also be a curse — like a double-edged sword that both nourishes and destroys the rice fields.

Mazandaran’s weather is moody. When the fog rises, it veils the grandeur of the mountains. And when rain pours, it may hammer roofs for days and nights without pause. At first, rain is the herald of life — but if it overstays, it changes face. Just like a guest: even the dearest one becomes a burden if they linger too long.

During the harvest season, if rain doesn’t cease, the rice rots and a farmer’s year-long labor vanishes. And what grief is heavier than unrewarded toil? A farmer, bare before nature, finds refuge only in heart and prayer.

But for us children, rain was a stubborn opponent. It stole our games, our running, our swims in the river, and our wanderings in the woods. We longed for the sun. That constant drizzle, that endless mist, turned the sky into a heavy ceiling.

On one of those endlessly rainy days, an old woman — her face as furrowed and wise as Takam’s plowed earth — looked at us and said:
“Dear hearts, why don’t you do Mah-Mah Khoto?”

We laughed at first. Could a song summon the sun? But that very day, we gave in to the old tradition. The village children lined up. We stepped onto the muddy ground, brimming with excitement, and went door to door. Homes without doors, yet full of heart. The only barrier was the “Losh” — short, latticed gates with long thin beams or wooden boards, strong enough to block cows and horses, but never eager children.

Meh mah mah khoto, mah mah khoto,
pirzena kel-pesht bakhete,
bamshi vene pesht bakhete,
khorshid khanoom aftab kon,
khorshid khanoom aftab kon,
Meh mah mah khoto, mah mah khoto,
The old woman sleeps beside the hearth’s warm glow,
While the cat curls softly, resting just below,
Lady Sun, shine for us,
Lady Sun, shine for us!

And then we’d cheer, we’d dance, and sing the same song again! That chant held more than words — it carried a plea for life. Little hearts bore great messages. Every woman who stepped out of her house would offer her best prayers, and sing with us as though reciting a sacred scripture.

And the lesson is this: a child who sings with hope is the greatest vessel for prayer.

Sometimes our reward was a handful of rice, a few eggs, a sweet, some chocolate, or a piece of chicken from the kitchen’s depths. But the value wasn’t in the amount — it lay in the kindness they were wrapped in. Children, like angels, are generous even in receiving.

That day, after the ceremony, still in the rain, we ventured into the wet forest. We made our little feast — built a fire, boiled the eggs, mixed the rice with salt and wild thyme. A meal sweeter than any banquet — not because of the taste, but because of the joy, the friendship, and the hope it held.

And strangely, a few days later, the rain stopped. Not by miracle, but as if in answer to kindness. The farmers, smiling, took their sickles and reaped the fields.

So now, years later, I say:
Culture lives in the songs of children calling the sun. Faith dwells in a woman handing rice to a child. And prayer may rise not from the altar, but from the alleys — beside the Losh gates.

And you, dear reader — if one day you find yourself in a land where the rain won’t stop, and hearts are weary, and hope is faint,
call the children. Let their song make the sun smile. Perhaps the sky still answers the pure-hearted.

And let the final wisdom be this:
Revive hearts with songs, not shouts.
And know: the sun must rise in hearts before it shines in the sky.
