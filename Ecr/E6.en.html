Connections between the Alborz and the Alps

Sometimes the bonds between nations do not pass through the iron gates of politics, nor are they heard in the resounding drums of power, but rather in a breeze that flows through a small garden, in the aroma rising from freshly baked bread, in the song of a family gathered around a fire. It is then that two distant lands, without maps ever meeting, become kindred in the depths of the soul.

Mazandaran in Iran and France, though far apart on the globe, breathe in a shared spirit: in rural farming that lives with the seasons, in food that recalls the memory of soil and sun, in the preservation of the mother tongue that lingers like a lullaby in children’s ears.

Look at a village on the slopes of the Alps; the soft sun shines on the vineyards, elderly men and women return with baskets of grapes, placing their bread and cheese on the stone porch. Close your eyes and go to Mazandaran; among the morning mist and birdsong, men and women set a pot of yogurt soup over the fire. A girl returns from the garden, and her grandfather hums a poetic phrase in their mother tongue.

In both places, the land is not just soil, but part of the human body. The rice paddies of Mazandaran and the vineyards of France convey the same truth: trees and crops are keepers of memory, reminding us of the scent of seasons and the taste of time. Harvesting is not just labor, but a celebration—celebration of solidarity, of being together.

Cooking in these cultures tells a profound story: just as the French patiently craft cheese or ratatouille, Mazandaranis grind walnuts, gather wild herbs, and bring dishes like Naz Khatoun and stuffed chicken to the table with love and cooperation. Every bite narrates a tale of the land, and every dish is a living map of geography.

Families in both lands are the pillars of life. In France, a grandmother tells stories with a local accent; in Mazandaran, a mother lulls her child to sleep in the Mazandarani language. The mother tongue—be it regional French or Mazandarani—is the language of love and song, not merely books.

Houses with gardens also share a similar face: fruit trees, a simple porch, chickens roaming the yard, chairs arranged toward the sunset. In Normandy or Kelijanrastaq, life still makes sense through the scent of fresh wood, the sound of streams, and genuine laughter.

Rituals, like streams of memory, connect generations: the grape harvest festival in France, or Norouz and Tirmah 13-sho in Mazandaran, are celebrated not from obligation but from heartfelt desire. They remind humans that the past is not a prison, but a bridge to tomorrow.

The nature of Mazandaran, with its Hyrcanian forests, relentless rains, and misty mountains, resembles the green lands of France more than the deserts of Iran. Just as Normandy or the Pyrenees thrive with humidity and rain, Mazandaran flourishes with mist and rivers.

People in Mazandaran are usually simple, modest, and honest. Integrity in work, purity in gaze, and grounded behavior still live in them. Though social changes have altered their lives, one can still visit a house in Dodangeh or Gahrobaran, sharing local bread and yogurt, and find hosts whose warmth is genuine, not performative. For many travelers or migrants, this recalls the simple, sincere people of French villages: living quietly, valuing work, welcoming guests, and cherishing an unpretentious life.

In Mazandaran, manual labor is honorable, just like in French villages; this is the secret of the closeness of these two cultures: being rooted in the soil, respecting labor, and humility in social life.

Thus, Mazandaran is not just a part of Iran, but a piece of a shared world; a world where humans can still trust one another, learn from nature, and find life in simplicity.

And here the thought should go further: what connects Mazandaran and France so deeply can flow between any two nations. Fundamental values—honesty, humility, solidarity, respect for the land, and love for language and home—know no borders or ethnicity. They are treasures hidden in the human spirit, whether in northern Iran, the heart of Europe, or anywhere in the world.

In a world where borders can be walls or bridges, the truth is: “Sustainable bridges are not built only from stone, wood, or iron; they are made of bread and poetry, of helping hands and compassionate hearts. And these are the bridges that bring people together, regardless of distance between lands.”
