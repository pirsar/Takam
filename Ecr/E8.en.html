The Soul of Tireng

In the days of youth, when my heart was unburdened and my eyes unclouded, and the morning breeze bore tidings of spring, I was once in Takam—a land delightful where the air smells of mountain herbs and meadows, and the views free the soul from worldly whispers.

I had a friend there, gentle and light-spirited, always smiling and pure of heart. One day, he borrowed his father’s hunting rifle and said, “O dear companion, since the weather is fair and our hearts inclined, let us walk to the Hemand grove—a vast field of wheat and open plains that gladden the soul.”

I agreed, not for the hunt but for his company and the beauty of the landscape. His dog joined us, running and attentive.

When we arrived, the sun kissed the wheat heads, and the breeze gently stirred the grass. My heart felt as light as a leaf in the wind. We wandered through the fields until we reached a secluded grove—greener than dreams, quieter than secrets.

Just then, a bird fluttered from a branch, singing sweetly. From among the tall boughs appeared no ordinary bird, but a marvel—Tireng! O son, if you’ve ever seen a rainbow in the mist or silk floating in the wind, you’ve seen a Tireng. A male pheasant, radiant with feathers like a thousand mirrors, each one reflecting eternal beauty.

Not just any bird—for if reason had wings, it would fly like this. A male Tireng, its feathers like colored silk, as though nature had gathered its last ounce of beauty and sealed it within.

I stared in awe, losing myself. It was as if the spirit of nature had descended into that bird, and the grove held its breath in reverence.

But suddenly, without a sound or sign, that beauty was gone—like a fallen star. Tireng, that wondrous bird, dropped from the sky like an autumn leaf, and my world darkened.

My friend cried out with joy, holding the enchanting creature’s body and spinning it in the air. “Behold! My first hunt!” he exclaimed.

But I stood silent and sorrowful. The beauty still shimmered in its feathers, but the soul had departed—like a crystal goblet shattered, yet the painted flowers still visible on its shards.

He said, “Let us cook and feast tonight on its meat.” I replied, “No, my soul finds no comfort in a meal that has cost a life.” And since that day, though years have passed, whenever I hear a bird’s song in a forest, I remember Tireng—and my heart trembles, and I whisper to myself:

“If beauty pleases you, preserve it—do not break it. If a bird sings sweetly, let it not fall silent for fear of a bullet. What is the difference between a lover of beauty and an enemy of life?”

And if I may offer a lesson:

“Humans ought to cherish life, not destroy it. If a hunter finds joy in killing, the bird too has a right to live. And those who stain their hands with the blood of nature shall one day find nature withdrawing her mercy. So, O you who hold a gun—if one day your finger rests on the trigger and your eyes on a deer, look into your heart first. Does this fleeting thrill outweigh the eternal regret?

O soul, was it worth taking a life for a moment of excitement? Does one who kills beauty not mar their own soul?

Let man not hunt except the moments—those that bring kindness and care. His true art lies in creating timeless moments, not in destroying life.

Birds of the air were not made for slaughter, but perhaps for their songs, their beauty, and the message of grace they carry. Whoever casts the shadow of his arrow over a bird's wing shall soon feel the shadow of sorrow upon his own heart.”

And this was the tale, O mindful soul—if you find a lesson in it, treasure it.

Hassan Mousavi Takami
Paris
June 2025
